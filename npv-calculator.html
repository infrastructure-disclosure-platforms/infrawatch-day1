Net present value · does the project pay for itself?

Testing whether a public project pays for itself

NPV compares what the public pays with what it gets back, future money pulled to today’s value. Read the result as a policy message, not a yes or no.

1Money today beats money tomorrow.

A shilling spent now could have earned interest, so a shilling returned in ten years is worth less than one returned today. NPV accounts for that gap with a discount rate.

2Pull the future back, then compare.

Pull every future cost and benefit back to today’s value, add them up, and set the total against what the public paid up front. If the discounted benefits clear the costs, the project pays for itself.

3Only as honest as the disclosure.

NPV runs on the financing terms, costs and revenues that are actually disclosed. Where the loan terms are sealed, the test cannot see the poison. The limit is disclosure, not the maths.

The inputs

Four things from the project record. Not sure about a field? Open its ? help for what to enter and where to find it.

?

What: The calendar year the government signed for the money. This is “year zero” and is fixed, you do not change it.

Why it matters: Counting from the signing year, not the opening year, means any delay or abandonment is still counted as a cost the public already bore.

Where to find it: The loan agreement or financing contract date.

Fixed at year zero. Every cash flow below is counted from this year.
?

What: One number per year, starting at the signing year. Money the public pays is a minus; money or savings that comes back is a plus.

Worked example: the public pays 1000 up front, then gets 400 back each year for three years:

-1000, 400, 400, 400

Where to find it: The feasibility study or the project’s disclosed financial model (construction cost, then projected tolls, fees or savings).

Comma-separated. The first number is the up-front cost, as a minus.
?

What: The yearly rate used to shrink future money back to today’s value, as a percentage.

Why it matters: A higher rate makes future benefits count for less, so it is harder for a project to pay for itself. Small changes can flip the verdict.

Where to find it: Use the government’s long-term (about 20-year) bond rate at the time the deal was signed. If unsure, 8–12% is a common range.

%
?

What: A money figure for benefits that are not direct revenue, jobs created, time saved, accidents avoided. Leave it at 0 if none is disclosed.

Why it matters: It is easy to inflate. This tool counts it but caps it at 15% of the cost, so a weak project cannot be rescued by vague “development” claims.

Where to find it: A social impact assessment or the project’s own benefit claims, in today’s money.

Only changes the wording of a red result: a useful asset with bad financing (poisoned chalice) reads differently from an asset that does not earn its cost (white elephant).
